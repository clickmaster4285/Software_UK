# **What Is UX Research?**

**UX research** is the structured study of users, their needs, behaviours, goals and interactions with a product or service.

Research can help answer questions such as:

* Who are our users?  
* What are they trying to achieve?  
* What problems do they experience?  
* Why are they abandoning a task?  
* Is our navigation understandable?  
* Does the proposed workflow make sense?  
* Can users complete important actions?  
* Which features matter most?  
* Are we solving the correct problem?  
* What should we improve first?

The aim is to produce evidence that supports product, UX and business decisions.

# **UX Research vs User Research**

The terms **UX research** and **user research** are often used interchangeably, particularly when researching digital experiences.

There can, however, be a slight difference in scope.

| UX Research | User Research |
| ----- | ----- |
| Often focused on product experience | Can cover a broader user context |
| Evaluates journeys and interfaces | Explores needs, behaviours and motivations |
| Frequently supports UX/UI decisions | Can influence product and service strategy |
| Includes usability testing | Can include wider exploratory research |
| Often evaluates digital products | May investigate experiences beyond a specific interface |

For this service, both approaches work toward the same objective: **understanding users well enough to make better product decisions**.

# **Our UX Research Services**

## **User Interviews**

User interviews help uncover what people think, need, expect and experience.

Interviews can explore:

* User goals  
* Motivations  
* Existing behaviour  
* Expectations  
* Frustrations  
* Current processes  
* Decision-making  
* Product perceptions  
* Unmet needs

The purpose is to understand **why they behave as they do and what problem sits behind the request**.

DotCentric, for example, combines interviews with usability testing to uncover how users think, behave and make decisions while using digital experiences.

## **Usability Testing**

Usability testing evaluates how easily users can complete tasks within:

* Websites  
* Mobile apps  
* Web applications  
* SaaS products  
* Prototypes  
* Ecommerce journeys  
* Digital services

A testing session might ask a participant to complete a realistic task while the researcher observes:

* Where they hesitate  
* What they misunderstand  
* Where they expect something different  
* Which errors occur  
* Whether the task is completed  
* What causes friction

The relationship is:

**User attempts task**

→ **Researcher observes behaviour**

→ **Usability issue identified**

→ **Cause investigated**

→ **Recommendation created**

Usability testing is one of the most consistently associated services in the UX research SERP.

## **Prototype Testing**

You do not always need a finished product before conducting research.

Clickable prototypes can be tested before engineering begins.

Prototype testing can help assess:

* Navigation  
* User flows  
* Terminology  
* Information hierarchy  
* Feature understanding  
* Form interactions  
* Onboarding  
* Checkout  
* Important product journeys

Finding an issue in a prototype can be less disruptive than finding the same issue after development.

For projects still at the design stage, research findings can feed into our [**digital product design services**](https://clickmasterssoftwaredevelopmentcompany.co.uk/design-ui-ux/product-design).

## **Surveys**

Surveys can collect structured feedback from a broader group of users.

Depending on the research objective, surveys may explore:

* Satisfaction  
* Preferences  
* Usage patterns  
* Product perceptions  
* Common problems  
* Expectations  
* Reasons for completing or abandoning an action

Surveys can help identify patterns, but the quality of the findings depends heavily on:

* The questions asked  
* The participants selected  
* Sample quality  
* Question wording  
* Analysis

They are most useful when selected for a clear research question rather than simply because they are easy to distribute.

## **Card Sorting**

Card sorting can help understand how users naturally group and categorise information.

It is particularly useful when researching:

* Website navigation  
* Product menus  
* Information architecture  
* Category structures  
* Feature organisation  
* Content hierarchy

For example:

**Business-defined navigation**

may not match:

**User mental model**

Card sorting can provide evidence for restructuring the information around how users expect to find it.

The SERP explicitly associates card sorting with UX research and information-architecture decisions.

## **Diary Studies**

Diary studies collect information about user behaviour over a longer period.

They can be useful when an experience:

* Happens repeatedly  
* Cannot be understood in one session  
* Depends on context  
* Changes over time  
* Involves multiple stages  
* Happens outside a controlled research setting

Participants may record relevant:

* Activities  
* Decisions  
* Problems  
* Thoughts  
* Context  
* Experiences

This can help reveal patterns that a single interview might not uncover.

## **Contextual & Ethnographic Research**

Some product questions require understanding users in the environment where behaviour actually occurs.

Contextual research can examine:

* How people perform existing tasks  
* Which tools they already use  
* Where workarounds occur  
* Which environmental factors influence behaviour  
* How people interact with other systems or people

Cyber-Duck includes ethnographic and diary-study techniques in its research methodology, alongside interviews, usability testing and quantitative studies.

## **UX Audits**

A UX audit evaluates an existing digital experience to identify potential usability problems.

The review may consider:

* Navigation  
* User flows  
* Forms  
* Information hierarchy  
* Calls to action  
* Search  
* Filters  
* Mobile behaviour  
* Interface consistency  
* Error handling  
* Product states  
* Accessibility considerations

A UX audit is particularly useful when you already know the product has problems but are unsure where they originate.

# **Generative vs Evaluative UX Research**

UX research can broadly be divided into **generative** and **evaluative** research.

## **Generative Research**

Generative research is used to discover and understand.

It asks:

**What problem should we solve?**

Typical questions include:

* What do users need?  
* What are they trying to accomplish?  
* What problems exist today?  
* What opportunities are being missed?  
* How do users currently behave?

Methods can include:

* Interviews  
* Contextual research  
* Diary studies  
* Exploratory research

## **Evaluative Research**

Evaluative research examines something that already exists.

It asks:

**Does this solution work for users?**

The subject could be:

* Wireframe  
* Prototype  
* Website  
* Mobile app  
* SaaS platform  
* Existing workflow

Methods can include:

* Usability testing  
* Prototype testing  
* UX audits  
* Surveys  
* Benchmarking

Google's current UX research interpretation explicitly distinguishes generative research for discovering needs and opportunities from evaluative research for assessing products, prototypes and interfaces.

# **Qualitative vs Quantitative UX Research**

Another useful distinction is between **qualitative** and **quantitative** research.

| Qualitative Research | Quantitative Research |
| ----- | ----- |
| Explores why something happens | Measures what or how often |
| Usually smaller participant groups | Can use larger datasets |
| Interviews | Surveys |
| Observation | Analytics |
| Usability sessions | Quantitative testing |
| Detailed behavioural insight | Numerical patterns |

For example:

**Analytics:**  
 40% of users abandon the process.

**Research:**  
 Users may reveal *why* they abandon it.

The two forms of evidence can therefore complement each other.

# **Which UX Research Method Do You Need?**

The correct method depends on the question you are trying to answer.

| Research Question | Possible Method |
| ----- | ----- |
| We don't understand the problem | Generative interviews |
| We want to understand user motivations | User interviews |
| Users cannot complete a journey | Usability testing |
| We need to test a proposed design | Prototype testing |
| Navigation is confusing | Card sorting |
| We need broader structured feedback | Survey |
| Behaviour changes over time | Diary study |
| We need to understand users in context | Contextual research |
| We need to diagnose an existing product | UX audit |

This method-selection content is an opportunity because much of the current SERP lists methods without clearly helping buyers decide which method fits which problem.

## **6\. Conduct the Research**

Sessions are then carried out using the selected methodology.

Individual observations are reviewed to identify:

* Repeated behaviours  
* Problems  
* Needs  
* Patterns  
* Contradictions  
* Opportunities  
* Usability issues

## **8\. Prioritise Findings**

Not every observation has the same importance.

Findings can be considered according to:

* Frequency  
* Severity  
* User impact  
* Business impact  
* Evidence strength

## **9\. Create Recommendations**

Research findings can then inform:

* Product decisions  
* UX priorities  
* User journeys  
* Information architecture  
* Interface changes  
* Prototype iterations  
* Development priorities

## **10\. Validate Improvements**

Where appropriate, revised designs can be tested again.

The process becomes:

**Research → Design → Test → Learn → Improve**

# **Frequently Asked Questions**

### **What are UX research services?**

UX research services help businesses understand users' needs, behaviours, motivations and problems through structured research methods such as interviews, usability testing, surveys, card sorting, diary studies and product evaluation.

### **What does UX research do?**

UX research generates evidence about users and their interaction with products or services. That evidence can help teams identify problems, validate assumptions and make better product and design decisions.

### **What is the difference between UX research and UX design?**

UX research investigates users, needs and behaviours. UX design uses those insights alongside product and business requirements to structure journeys, interactions and experiences.

### **What is the difference between user research and UX research?**

The terms often overlap. User research can investigate users more broadly, while UX research commonly focuses on how users interact with products, services and digital experiences.

### **What UX research methods can be used?**

Depending on the research question, methods can include user interviews, usability testing, prototype testing, surveys, card sorting, diary studies, contextual research and UX audits.

### **What is generative UX research?**

Generative research explores user needs, behaviours, problems and opportunities before a specific solution has necessarily been defined.

### **What is evaluative UX research?**

Evaluative research tests an existing concept, interface, prototype or product to understand how effectively it works for users.

### **When should UX research be conducted?**

UX research can be conducted during discovery, product planning, design, prototyping, development, after launch and during ongoing optimisation.

### **Can UX research test a prototype?**

Yes. Prototype testing can help evaluate user flows, navigation, interactions and understanding before full software development.

### **Can UX research improve an existing product?**

Research can identify usability problems, pain points and user needs that can inform improvements to an existing website, app or software product.

### **How much does UX research cost?**

Cost depends on the research objectives, methods, participant requirements, recruitment, number of sessions, product complexity, analysis and required deliverables.

### **How long does a UX research project take?**

The timeline depends on the research question, methodology, participant recruitment, number of sessions, analysis and deliverables.

### **What do you receive after UX research?**

Depending on scope, deliverables can include research findings, usability issues, user insights, journey maps, prioritised findings, opportunity areas and recommendations for product or design decisions.
